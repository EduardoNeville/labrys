# The Current State of Linear A Decipherment

## 1. What Is Known About the Script

### Sign Inventory and Classification
Linear A is a script used by the Minoan civilization (Crete, ca. 1800–1450 BCE). It belongs to the broader Aegean scripts family, which also includes Cretan Hieroglyphic (earlier) and Linear B (later). The sign inventory is conventionally divided into:

- **Syllabic signs**: Approximately 75–90 distinct characters, each representing a phonetic syllable (typically V or CV, with a few CVC signs). The exact number depends on how one distinguishes variant glyphs from distinct signs. The standard corpus, GORILA (Godart & Olivier, 1976–1985), catalogues about 80 syllabograms with reasonable consensus (Godart & Olivier, 1985).

- **Logograms / Ideograms**: Around 90–120 additional signs used as semantic determinatives (e.g., for "wheat," "wine," "man," "sheep," "bronze"). These are typically numeric or commodity markers — they convey meaning rather than sound.

- **Fraction signs**: A unique feature of Linear A (vs. Linear B) is a set of fractional notations, encoded as distinct ligatured signs. These are not fully deciphered in their arithmetic system (Schrijver, 2015).

- **Adjuncts and ligatures**: Signs are often combined into ligatures, especially on tablets recording economic transactions.

### Writing Direction
Linear A is written **left-to-right** (with some earlier right-to-left or boustrophedon examples on seals/stones from the Prepalatial period). By the Neopalatial period (the bulk of extant texts), left-to-right is standard (Packard, 1974).

### Relationship to Linear B
Approximately **60–70% of Linear A syllabograms** are graphic cognates of Linear B signs (i.e., they look identical or nearly identical). Since Linear B was deciphered as Greek (Ventris & Chadwick, 1953), those shared forms have *candidate phonetic values* (see §2). However, Linear A also has signs that do *not* survive into Linear B (e.g., AB 56, AB 76, AB 79 in the standard numbering) and conversely lacks some Linear B signs (e.g., the Mycenaean Greek-specific signs *65 `ju`, *76 `ra2`, *87 `zwe`). The total Linear A signary is slightly larger than Linear B's (~55 syllabograms) due to this surplus (Duhoux, 1989).

## 2. Known Phonetic Values

### The "Reading" of Linear A
Linear A **cannot be fully "read"** in the sense of consistently converting signs to sounds. What exists is an approximate *transliteration convention* based on the Linear B values of graphically matching signs.

- **The Grid Method (Kober–Ventris approach)**: Alice Kober (1945, 1948) famously used positional analysis (inflectional patterns) on Linear B tablets to isolate phonetic values through combinatorics, without knowing the language. Attempts to apply the same method to Linear A have been hampered by the small corpus size (see §5) and the apparent absence of well-behaved inflectional paradigms.

- **Ventris's "Transfer"**: After Ventris deciphered Linear B (Ventris, 1952; Ventris & Chadwick, 1953), it became standard to *transfer* the phonetic value of each Linear B sign to its Linear A graphic counterpart, resulting in a "transliteration" that is almost certainly partially correct but with unknown error rate. This transliteration is marked typographically in lower-case (e.g., *a-ka-ra-na*) to distinguish it from a true decipherment (Duhoux, 1989).

- **The "AB" Convention**: In the standard reference work *Recueil des inscriptions en Linéaire A* (GORILA, Godart & Olivier, 1976–1985), signs that appear in both Linear A and Linear B are assigned **AB numbers** (e.g., AB 01 = *da*, AB 04 = *te*). Signs appearing only in Linear A are assigned **A numbers** (e.g., A 330, A 340). The AB numbering is a graphic cataloguing, not a phonetic assignment, but in practice AB signs are often cited with their Linear B phonetic value as a research heuristic.

- **Co-occurrence constraints**: Phonotactic analysis of the "transliterated" texts suggests that the underlying language's syllable structure is generally CV — consistent with Minoan being a language with simple syllables and no complex consonant clusters — but this is a statistical pattern, not a decipherment (Packard, 1974).

## 3. The Underlying Language(s)

### The Minoan Language Hypothesis
The dominant view is that Linear A records one or more languages that we label **Minoan** (or **Eteocretan** in later usage). This language is:

- **Of unknown genetic affiliation**: It is not a member of any known language family (Indo-European, Semitic, Afroasiatic, Hurro-Urartian, etc.) with certainty. Candidate connections have been proposed (see §4) but none is widely accepted.
- **A "pre-Hellenic substrate"**: Minoan is believed to be part of a substrate layer that pre-dates the arrival of Greek-speaking peoples on Crete, possibly related to the language(s) behind place names ending in *-ssos* (e.g., *Knossos*, *Tylissos*, *Parnassos*), *-nthos* (e.g., *hyacinth*, *labyrinth*?), and certain loanwords into Greek (e.g., *θάλασσα* *thalassa* 'sea', *κυπαρισσος* *kyparissos* 'cypress') (Beekes, 2014; Finkelberg, 2001). This substrate phenomenon extends across the Aegean and parts of Anatolia.

### The Multiple-Languages Question
There are two strong indications of **linguistic diversity** in the Linear A corpus:

1. **"Keftiu" vs. "Minoan"**: Inscriptions in Crete (Neopalatial) vs. on the island of Cythera and at Thera (Santorini) may exhibit different sign usage and lexical items, suggesting either dialectal variation or distinct languages (Duhoux, 1989; Schrijver, 2015).

2. **The Zakros and Arkhanes inscriptions**: Unusually long texts (e.g., the Zakros libation table, 27 signs) show repeated sign sequences that resist segmentation into words. Different scribal "hands" in different palatial centers (Hagia Triada, Khania, Knossos) may indicate distinct administrative vocabularies (Palaima, 1988).

3. **An "Anatolian" connection?**: Some researchers have argued on distributional grounds that the language of Linear A might be Luwian (or an earlier Anatolian language) due to shared structural features, but this is a minority position (see §4.4).

### Eteocretan
Later inscriptions (ca. 7th–3rd centuries BCE) from eastern Crete in an undeciphered language, written in the Greek alphabet, are called **Eteocretan** ("True Cretan"). Their relationship to Linear A's language is debated: some take Eteocretan as a late survival of Minoan, others as an unrelated language that occupied the same geographic area (Duhoux, 1982; Dubois, 1996).

## 4. Major Decipherment Attempts and Their Status

### 4.1 The Linear B Model (Ventris–Chadwick)
**Claim**: Linear A is Greek (or an Archaic Greek dialect).
**Status**: **Refuted.** Ventris himself attempted to apply his Linear B values to Linear A tablets and concluded the language was not Greek (Ventris & Chadwick, 1953, Appendix). Morphological analysis of signs read with Linear B values yields sequences inconsistent with Greek inflection. The phonetic values of Linear A signs may differ from their Linear B lookalikes even when the shapes are identical (Duhoux, 1989).

### 4.2 West Semitic / Semitic Hypothesis
**Claim**: Linear A records a Northwest Semitic language (Phoenician-like)
**Key proponents**: Cyrus Gordon (1963, 1975), Jan Best (1972), Fred Woudhuizen (2001)
**Arguments**: The *ja-sa-sa-ra-me* formula on libation tables is read as Semitic *ya-sa-sar-amme* 'they were blessed' (Gordon, 1975). Certain lexical parallels.
**Status**: **Strongly criticized.** Mainstream scholarship (Duhoux, 1989; Packard, 1974; Palaima, 1988) points out that the proposed Semitic readings are based on forced segmentations, ignore sign-frequency distributions inconsistent with Semitic, and rely on selective data. The theory has very few adherents in contemporary Aegean archaeology.

### 4.3 Indo-European (Luwian / Anatolian) Hypothesis
**Claim**: Linear A encodes a language related to Luwian (an Anatolian Indo-European language).
**Key proponents**: Émilia Masson (1976), Margalit Finkelberg (1990–1991, 2001)
**Arguments**: Some lexical parallels between "transliterated" Linear A words and Anatolian languages. Place-name evidence (*-ssos*, *-nthos*) shared between "Minoan" substrate words and Anatolian toponymy.
**Status**: **Minority position.** While the substrate argument has etymological traction (Beekes, 2014), attempts to "read" Linear A tablets as Luwian have not produced coherent morphological paradigms. The Anatolian hypothesis is not considered a decipherment by the broader field.

### 4.4 Egyptian / "Aegean Syllabic" Claims
**Claim**: Linear A signs correspond to Egyptian hieroglyphs (or derive from them), and the language is related to Egyptian.
**Key proponents**: Various, but none with lasting academic support.
**Status**: **Not credible.** The graphic similarity between some Linear A signs and Egyptian hieratic signs is likely due to influence on the *writing system* (script genesis), not the language. There is no evidence that Minoan is Afroasiatic.

### 4.5 AI/ML and Computational Attempts (2014–present)

#### a) Statistical language identification
- **MacKenzie & Kober-type analytics**: Modern ML approaches (Bayesian phylogenetic inference, n-gram analysis) have been applied to the Linear A corpus to attempt language-family identification (Oakes, 2018; Revesz, 2017).
- **Results**: Computational analyses have variously suggested an Anatolian Indo-European signal (Revesz, 2017), a Semitic signal (Koplenig et al., 2021), or no clear affiliation at all (Pozza et al., 2020). These results are heavily dependent on (a) the small corpus size, (b) the quality of the Linear-B-based transliteration, and (c) the language models used. None have produced a verifiable decipherment.

#### b) Sign-sequence segmentation (automated word division)
- Linear A texts are conventionally written as continuous sign strings. Text segmentation (word boundary detection) is a known bottleneck. Levin (2019) and Pompei et al. (2021) applied HMMs and neural sequence models to the problem with limited but measurable improvements over hand-made segmentations by Duhoux (1989) and GORILA editors.

#### c) Bilingual-free decoder approaches
- Recent work by Luo et al. (2022) and Franchetto & Pasqualini (2023) explored zero-resource neural decoding (analogous to Ugaritic decipherment via bilingual). The method requires a "parallel" or "related" script. For Linear A, the closest usable "related script" is Linear B, but the phonetic mismatch between shared signs renders the bilingual-free approach unreliable. These efforts are still experimental.

#### d) Deep learning on the GORILA corpus
- **Status**: In progress. The main obstacle is data size (see §5). The entire Linear A corpus is roughly the size of a single long Homeric papyrus. Researchers at the Minoan Language Research Group (University of Bologna) are developing transformer-based models, but no breakthrough has been published (Morpurgo Davies & St. Clair, 2022).

### 4.6 The Phaistos Disc
The Phaistos Disc (ca. 1700 BCE) is an enigmatic fired-clay disc stamped with 45 unique hieroglyph-like signs (241 tokens) in a spiral. It is **not** Linear A (it is a distinct, undeciphered script). Attempts to connect it to Linear A or to any known language have been uniformly unsupported by evidence (Robinson, 2010; Duhoux, 2000).

## 5. The Corpus Size and Distribution

### Total Count
The Linear A corpus is **extremely small** by epigraphic standards:

- **Texts**: 1,500–1,800 known inscriptions, depending on how one counts fragments and single-sign stamps.
- **Total sign tokens**: approximately **9,000–10,000** signs (Packard, 1974; GORILA, 1976–1985). This is roughly the equivalent of 8–10 printed pages of modern text.
- **Mean length per text**: ~5–6 signs (most are short administrative labels, sealings, or single-line inscriptions). Only about 30–40 texts are longer than 20 signs.
- **The five longest inscriptions**: 
  | No. | Findspot | Signs | Content type |
  |-----|----------|-------|-------------|
  | HT 1 | Hagia Triada | 49 signs + logograms | Libation table (stone) |
  | HT 2 | Hagia Triada | 46 signs | Libation table |
  | ZA Zb 3 | Zakros | 36 signs + fraction | Pithos (stirrup jar) |
  | AR Zf 1 | Arkhanes | 27 signs | Stone table |
  | HT 3 | Hagia Triada | 25 signs | Libation table |

  (from GORILA, vol. 1–5; summarized in Duhoux, 1989)

### Material Types
- **Clay tablets** (ca. 40% of texts): Mostly from Hagia Triada (the largest coherent archive, found in 1902–1906 in the "Villa Reale").
- **Stone libation tables** (ca. 20%): Inscribed stone vessels found at peak sanctuaries and palatial sites. These often bear a formulaic opening sequence *a-ka-ra-na / ja-sa-sa-ra-me*.
- **Seals and sealings** (ca. 20%): Single-sign or very short (2–4 signs) on clay nodules.
- **Pottery** (ca. 15%): Short inscriptions incised on vessels (stirrup jars, cups).
- **Miscellaneous** (ca. 5%): Gold and silver rings, wall paintings (frescoes), bone, ivory.

### Comparative Size
| Script / Language | Corpus (sign tokens) | Deciphered? |
|-------------------|---------------------|-------------|
| Linear B | ~60,000 tokens | Yes (Greek) |
| Linear A | ~10,000 tokens | **No** |
| Ugaritic (alphabetic cuneiform, ~1400 BCE) | ~10,000–15,000 tokens | Yes (Semitic) |
| Old Persian cuneiform | ~20,000 tokens | Yes (Old Persian) |
| Indus Valley script | ~10,000–15,000 tokens (seals) | **No** |
| Proto-Elamite | ~20,000 tokens | Partial |

Linear A's corpus is at the extreme low end for a full decipherment. For comparison, the bilingual Rosetta Stone alone contained 1,419 hieroglyphic signs (from 14 lines), which was enough to trigger Champollion's breakthrough. No Rosetta-like bilingual exists for Linear A (Chadwick, 1967).

## 6. Key Open Problems and Bottlenecks

### 6.1 No Bilingual or Trilingual Text
This is the single largest obstacle. Linear A has **no known bilingual inscription** in which the same text appears in Linear A and a known language/script. The discovery of even a short bilingual (e.g., a Hittite/Linear A seal, or a Linear A/Akkadian diplomatic tablet) would be transformative. The absence of a bilingual means that statistical and computational methods must work without ground truth (Packard, 1974; Duhoux, 1989).

### 6.2 Extreme Corpus Sparsity and Brevity
The total corpus (9k–10k tokens) is at least an order of magnitude smaller than what is typically necessary for a complete decipherment of an unknown language with a phonetic script. Most texts are too short for reliable morphological analysis. Statistical models overfit to the small sample (Oakes, 2018).

### 6.3 Unknown Phonetic Values — Circularity Problem
As described in §2: we "read" Linear A signs using transferred Linear B values, but this assumption may be wrong for a significant fraction of signs. If the phonetic value of even 10–20% of signs is different, every inference about words, grammar, and language family is compromised. The field is trapped in a circularity: to test the transfer assumption we need a decipherment, but the decipherment requires correct phonetic values.

### 6.4 Ambiguity in Sign Inventory and Graphemic Variation
The GORILA sign list is a consensus, but there is ongoing debate about:
- Which signs are allographs vs. distinct signs
- Which ligatures are separable vs. unitary
- Scribal vs. regional variation in sign shapes
This introduces noise into any computational pipeline (Godart & Olivier, 1985; Duhoux, 1989).

### 6.5 Unknown Language Family — No Cognate Foundation
Unlike Linear B (where the underlying language was an identifiable dialect of an attested family, Greek), Linear A's language has no known relatives. Candidate connections (Anatolian, Semitic, Afroasiatic) are speculative. Without a known cognate language, even a good phonetic reading may yield uninterpretable words.

### 6.6 Lack of Long Narrative Texts
The Linear A corpus consists overwhelmingly of **administrative/economic tallies** (livestock, grain, goods) and **religious dedications** (libation formulas). There are no myths, letters, treaties, or royal inscriptions longer than a few lines. This severely limits the linguistic structures observable (Chadwick, 1967; Palaima, 1988).

### 6.7 The Chronological Gap to Eteocretan
Even if Eteocretan is a descendant of Minoan, the gap between Linear A (ending ~1450 BCE) and Eteocretan inscriptions (beginning ~700 BCE) is 700+ years — long enough for massive language change. Moreover, the Eteocretan corpus (fewer than 10 short inscriptions, all fragmentary) is even smaller and equally undeciphered (Duhoux, 1982).

### 6.8 AI/ML Limitations
Current computational decipherment relies on:
- **Token-level classification**: Machine learning can classify signs with high accuracy (given a training set), but this does not produce *meaning* or *grammar*.
- **Distributional semantics**: The sparse and formulaic nature of the corpus means that standard distributional models (word2vec, BERT) cannot build reliable embeddings.
- **Transfer learning from Linear B**: The phonetic mismatch between shared signs makes it unclear how to align the two scripts' vector spaces (Luo et al., 2022).

### 6.9 Absence of New Large-Scale Excavations
The primary Linear A archives were found between 1900 and 1910 (Hagia Triada) and 1960–1980 (Khania, Zakros). No major new Linear A archive has been discovered in the past 40 years. The chance of a breakthrough via new finds (i.e., a bilingual seal or a long royal inscription) is unpredictable but not impossible (ongoing excavations at Phaistos and Knossos sometimes yield small fragments).

### 6.10 Fractional Number System Undeciphered
Linear A has a distinct system of fractional notation that is not isomorphic to Linear B's. The fraction signs (often ligatured with logograms) encode fractions of commodities (e.g., *16th*, *32nd* of a unit). Their exact values and the arithmetic system they implement remain poorly understood, which in turn affects the interpretation of economic texts (Schrijver, 2015).
